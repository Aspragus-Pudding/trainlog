# Pre-op shoulder protocol

Written against your actual imaging, not a generic instability template.

**What the scans say:**
- Anterior and anteroinferior labral tear, medialized and scarred (ALPSA-type)
- Chronic Hill-Sachs, index 19.1 mm, glenoid track 20.8 mm — **on-track**, 1.7 mm
  of margin
- Glenoid bone deficiency 3% — negligible
- Rotator cuff completely intact, no tendinopathy, normal muscle signal
- Recurrent instability episodes

**What that means for this document.** Your cuff is clean, so nothing here is
treating a tendon problem. Your bone stock is good, so you can load hard. What
you've lost is the *passive* restraint at the front of the joint. No exercise
rebuilds it. Everything below is aimed at two things only:

1. **Not dislocating again before surgery.** Your track margin is 1.7 mm. Each
   episode can erode glenoid bone and narrow it, and an on-track lesion can
   become off-track — which is the difference between a Bankart repair and a
   bigger operation.
2. **Arriving at surgery as strong and muscular as possible.** Pre-op strength
   and muscle mass are among the better predictors of post-op outcome, and
   they're one of the few things you control.

Your physios' advice — avoid what hurts, build as much muscle as you can — is
the correct protocol. This is that advice made specific.

---

## The one rule that matters

**Anterior instability is vulnerable in abduction plus external rotation.**
Arm out to the side, rotated back, elbow behind the plane of the torso.

Everything on the avoid list is a version of that position under load. Everything
on the safe list either stays out of it or is loaded in a direction that pulls
the humeral head *back* into the socket rather than forward.

---

## Tier 1 — Dynamic stabilisation

Not for size. For control, endurance, and joint position sense. Load is low and
should stay low; these are not meant to be hard.

**3–4× per week, 10 minutes.**

| Exercise | Sets × reps | Note |
|---|---|---|
| External rotation at side, band or cable | 3 × 15–20 | Towel under the elbow. Arm stays at your side — **not** the 90/90 version |
| Side-lying external rotation | 2 × 15 | Same muscles, zero risk position |
| Internal rotation at side | 2 × 15 | Subscapularis is a front-of-joint stabiliser and often skipped |
| Isometric ER hold at neutral | 3 × 30 s | Build tolerance without movement |
| Prone T raise | 2 × 15 | Keep it low, thumbs up. Not a Y — that's overhead |
| Serratus punch | 2 × 15 | Scapular protraction, keeps the socket under the ball |
| Low trap raise, below 90° | 2 × 12–15 | Lower trap is a genuine scapular stabiliser and routinely skipped. Keep the arm under shoulder height — this is not a prone Y |
| Scaption raise, thumbs up | 2 × 12 | Scapular plane, stop at shoulder height. Anterior and lateral delt act as dynamic anterior restraints |
| Belly press | 2 × 15 | Subscapularis specifically — the largest front-of-joint stabiliser, and the one most often left out |

**One thing to actively avoid: aggressive capsular stretching.** Sleeper
stretches and end-range cross-body work get prescribed for stiff shoulders.
Yours is the opposite problem. You do not want more anterior laxity. Thoracic
extension over a roller is fine and worth 60 seconds — that's mobility above the
joint, not through it.

**Why not the 90/90 external rotation drill** everyone prescribes: it's the
at-risk position, just unloaded. It's the right exercise for a cuff problem. It
isn't the right exercise for yours. Worth raising with your physio rather than
dropping unilaterally, but it's a fair question to ask.

## Tier 2 — Proprioception and closed chain

Joint position sense is measurably impaired after instability episodes and it is
trainable. Closed-chain work is safe because the hand is fixed and the joint is
compressed rather than distracted.

**2–3× per week, 5 minutes.**

- Rhythmic stabilisation — hand on a wall, partner or band perturbs, you resist. 3 × 30 s
- **Joint position sense drill** — eyes closed, move the arm to a target position,
  open and check. 2 × 10. Position sense is measurably impaired after instability
  episodes and it is trainable, which is unusually well supported for something
  this cheap
- Quadruped weight shifts — 3 × 30 s
- Bear position hold — 3 × 20–30 s
- Wall push-up plus — 2 × 15
- Loaded carries — 3 × 30 m. Stabilisation under real load with no ROM at all

## Tier 3 — The actual muscle building

**This is where most of your effort should go.** Tiers 1 and 2 are maintenance;
this is the part that changes your post-op outcome.

Train heavy and progressively in pain-free ranges. Your cuff is intact — there's
no tissue reason to hold back on anything that isn't in the at-risk position.

**Safe and productive:**
- **All pulling.** Rows, pulldowns, chin-ups, face pulls. Pulling loads the joint
  in the opposite direction from the vulnerable one. Go hard.
- **Neutral-grip and fixed-path pressing.** Machine chest press, landmine press,
  floor press, neutral DB press.
- **Elbow-tucked barbell pressing.** Around 45°, moderate grip.
- **Everything lower body.** Unaffected entirely.
- **Direct arm and delt work**, keeping lateral raises at or below shoulder height.

## Avoid before surgery

**Red — the at-risk position under load:**
- Pec fly and cable crossover at end range
- Behind-the-neck press, behind-the-neck pulldown
- Deep dips
- Barbell pullover at end range
- Wide-grip bench with elbows flared to 90° at the bottom
- Dumbbell bench taken to full depth (dumbbells let you go past where a bar stops you)
- Snatch, overhead squat, kipping pull-ups

**Yellow — fine with a setup change:**
- Bench press: keep elbows near 45°, moderate grip
- Overhead pressing: loaded overhead is worth attention, landmine is the safer version
- Any dumbbell pressing: control the bottom, don't chase depth

**Not in the gym at all, and this is the important one: boxing and sparring.**
A missed hook is the at-risk position at speed with nothing to decelerate against,
and clinching means someone else applying force to your arm. Continuing to box
before surgery is the single most likely thing to make your surgery worse.
Nothing in the gym comes close.

## Nutrition, given where you are

- **Don't train hard in a meaningful deficit before surgery.** Pre-op muscle mass
  predicts post-op outcome, and energy restriction impairs recovery.
- **Protein at the high end**, roughly 1.6–2.2 g/kg.
- **Creatine, 3–5 g daily.** Good evidence it attenuates muscle loss during limb
  immobilisation and improves the rehab phase after. Cheap, well characterised,
  and it targets exactly your post-op problem.
- **Vitamin D if you're low.** Northern latitudes and indoor training make that likely.

## When to stop and call someone

- Another dislocation or subluxation, even one that self-reduces — **call the
  same week.** This is the event that can change your surgery.
- Pain bad enough to stop a session (level 4–5 in the app)
- Night pain that wakes you or stops you sleeping
- Any new numbness, weakness, or a sense of the joint slipping

## Questions for your surgeon

1. My Hill-Sachs index is 19.1 mm against a glenoid track of 20.8 mm — a
   distance-to-dislocation of about 1.7 mm. Does the **near-track** concept
   change your approach, specifically whether you'd add a **remplissage** to a
   Bankart repair?
2. Does the **ALPSA morphology** change the expected recurrence rate versus a
   standard Bankart?
3. Given recurrent episodes, how much does my risk profile change if I dislocate
   again before surgery?
4. I want to return to MMA including grappling. Does that change the procedure
   you'd recommend?
